About this comparator

The Progressive Lens Comparator shows how a progressive addition lens distributes power across its surface, and what that means for a particular wearer. Enter the glasses a patient is wearing and the prescription you have just measured, choose what they are looking at, and the two lenses are mapped side by side: where each one is clear, where it blurs, and what the eye has to do to compensate.

It was built for chairside conversations. Two of them come up every day. A patient whose current glasses are over-minused sees "fine" far away, but the map shows that the clear distance zone has slipped below the fitting cross, so they are lifting their chin to find the extra plus lower in the lens. And a small increase in plus at distance moves the whole corridor with it, so near vision improves too, which the sample-point powers show directly.

How to use it

  • Prescriptions: the new prescription is taken as the eye's true refraction, and everything is measured against it. Sphere, cylinder and axis are all modelled with power vectors, so an axis change alone produces blur.
  • Looking at: pick a viewing distance. The lens needs the distance prescription plus the dioptric demand of that distance, less whatever accommodation the patient can comfortably supply. "Add needed" shows the result.
  • Clear zones: the default map colours every point of each lens by what it brings into focus for this wearer: far, mid-range (about 1 m to a few metres) or reading (45 cm and closer), with the blurred sides of the progressive in grey and any area that is too strong for every distance in violet. The zone names are written on the lens, and the width of each zone is measured through the corridor in millimetres, so two designs can be compared at a glance.
  • Other maps: "Blur" combines the residual spherical error, the prescription's cylinder mismatch and the lens' own unwanted astigmatism into one blur strength at every point. "Unwanted astigmatism" is the lens design on its own, as a manufacturer's plot would show it. "Mean power vs need" shows how far the local power sits from what the eye needs, minus in blue and plus in red.
  • Fit and gaze: the fitting height and centration sliders move the lens relative to the eye. The gaze sliders move the pupil through the lens, and the pupil can also be dragged on any map. "Find clear view" finds the nearest posture that brings the chosen distance into focus and shows the chin lift it takes.
  • What the patient sees: a letter chart blurred by the residual error at the pupil, for the worse eye, with an approximate acuity.
  • Share: the address bar holds every setting, so "Copy link" reproduces exactly what you see. "Save image" downloads a labelled picture of both lenses.

The model, honestly

This is a teaching model, not ray tracing of a real lens file. The geometry is in millimetres: a 60 mm lens, fitting cross 7 mm above the geometric centre, 2.5 mm near inset, corridors of 11, 14 or 17 mm from the fitting cross to the full add, and 25 mm from the lens to the eye's centre of rotation. The add rises along the corridor as a smooth curve. Unwanted astigmatism grows with distance from the corridor centre, is strongest beside the corridor and in the near periphery, and scales with the add and the design's hardness; the local mean power drops by half of it, as in a real lens. Blur strength is the length of the power-vector difference between what the lens provides and what the eye needs, after accommodation has covered what it can; up to 0.30 D is treated as within the eye's depth of focus and counted as clear. Accommodation follows Hofstetter's formulae and is only used for a shortfall of plus, never for an excess. The named designs are illustrative families, not measured manufacturer data.

Not modelled: vertex distance and pantoscopic tilt, prism and Prentice-rule effects, lens thickness, chromatic effects, and the real asymmetry of manufacturer designs. The acuity estimate converts blur strength and pupil size to a Snellen fraction with a simple empirical rule and is approximate.
